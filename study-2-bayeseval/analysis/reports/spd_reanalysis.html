Cell by cell

What actually happens

Overconfident models 7 cells · robust

All four MedEval, WGD Gemini & Llama, LifeEval Gemini. Large ΔECE (−0.13 to −0.33), tight CIs, identical under both standards, p<0.001. SPD roughly halves ECE where models are badly overconfident. This is the real result.

GPT-5.4 Mini · WGD the "+89%" backfire → null

Not significant under either standard (pooled +0.035 p=.09; stratified −0.006 p=.71). Its honest DCE calibration already sat at SPD's WGD floor (~0.08–0.10) — nothing to fix, nothing broken.

Claude Haiku 4.5 · LifeEval the "+335%" backfire → metric split

Significantly worse under pooled ECE (+0.113) yet significantly better under stratified (−0.055) — same cell, opposite signs. DCE only wins marginally because pooling lets its per-difficulty errors cancel. Not "SPD hurts a well-calibrated model."

Llama 4 Maverick · LifeEval the one robust backfire

Worse under both standards (+0.069 pooled, +0.044 stratified, both p<0.001). Small but real — and never flagged in the paper. SPD's bin-pinned modal mass (0.27) turns underconfident against LifeEval's high base rate (~0.63).

Why the paper's numbers misled

Four confounds, each verified

C1 · percentage inflation

Tiny denominators explode

ΔECE% divides by the DCE baseline. The two "backfires" sit on the two smallest baselines in the table, so a modest absolute move becomes "+89%" / "+335%." Percent is not an effect size.

C2 · pooling cancellation

Standard ECE hides miscalibration

Binning by confidence alone lets over- and under-confidence at different difficulties cancel. DCE sweeps 20 levels, SPD 4. Stratified, Claude/LifeEval DCE goes 0.05 → 0.23.

C3 · answer-parse dropout

Half of Claude's DCE was discarded

Plain to_numeric drops "77 years" → NaN. Claude/LifeEval kept only 52% of DCE rows vs 100% of SPD. We re-score with the lenient extractor to recover them.

C4 · difficulty mismatch

The modes tested different mixes

DCE's 20 difficulty levels vs SPD's 4 make the marginal mixes differ. We restrict DCE to the shared {1,5,10,20} and pair on item identity (photo / age+sex / patient).

Mechanism

SPD's calibration is set by task structure

SPD's scored confidence is the modal-bin mass — pinned behaviourally by spreading probability across bins, roughly domain-fixed (≈0.30 across all WGD models). Its residual miscalibration is dominated by how far that level sits from the domain's base-rate accuracy (r = 0.99). So SPD calibrates worst exactly where its bin-pinned confidence mismatches the base rate — LifeEval — not where the model was "well-calibrated."

structural gap · one model×domain cell · SPD rows i = 1…N
gap = | m̄ − ȳ | = | (1/N) Σi ( confi − yi ) | = | mean over-confidence |
where  m̄ = (1/N) Σ confi (mean modal-bin mass)  ·  ȳ = (1/N) Σ yi (mean true_probability = base-rate accuracy)

It is mean-then-absolute: the signed over/under-confidence is averaged first, and the absolute value is taken only at the end — so it is SPD's net miscalibration, and by the triangle inequality a lower bound on ECE (ECE ≥ gap). In code: abs(mean_conf − accuracy) on the SPD frame.

Methods, in depth

The two ways to score calibration

Both "techniques" are the same primitive fed different rows — nothing else differs. Each scored row carries a confidence conf (DCE scalar, or SPD modal-bin mass), a ground-truth y, and a difficulty label. One function turns those into a number:

compute_ece(conf, y)
1  bin rows into 11 fixed confidence bins  [0,.1) … [.9,1) [1.0]
2  per bin b:  nb=count,  c̄b=mean conf,  ȳb=mean y
3  ECE = Σb (nb/N) · |ȳb − c̄b|

One wrinkle in the inputs: y is hit/miss (0 or 1) for WGD but a continuous correctness probability for LifeEval and MedEval — so a bin's "accuracy" is a hit-rate in one domain and a mean probability in the others.

Technique A

Pooled ECE — the paper's metric

Feed it: all rows together, one bin partition, one number. Estimates: marginal calibration — "over my whole track record, when I say p, is the outcome p?"

Steelman · textbook, pre-registered, no auxiliary variable, lowest variance. Exactly right if a consumer only wants "discount an 0.8 to 0.8."

Strawman · blind to a model that is overconfident on hard items and underconfident on easy ones — those cancel inside a confidence bin, which is the regime here (overconfidence rises with difficulty).

Justified when the decision is marginal or confidence is uncorrelated with difficulty. Not when confidence tracks difficulty and decisions condition on the item.

Technique B

Stratified — conditional on difficulty

Feed it: rows split by difficulty; ECE within each stratum, then count-weighted average. Estimates: conditional calibration — "within a difficulty, when I say p, is the outcome p?"

Steelman · calibration should hold conditional on what the decider knows; conditioning on a designed, observable difficulty axis removes an identifiable cancellation channel. Strictly stronger than pooled.

Strawman · removes cancellation only along the axis you stratify (a lower bound on true miscalibration); fewer rows/bin → upward small-sample bias; a researcher degree of freedom; changes the estimand.

Justified when a covariate correlated with confidence and outcome is observable and strata are large — all true here.

Why they can disagree — the cancellation identity

Because |mean| ≤ mean|·|, stratified ECE ≥ pooled ECE always, and the gap is exactly the cross-difficulty cancellation. One confidence bin (conf ≈ 0.5):

Pooled: ȳ = 0.5 = c̄ → ECE 0 ("perfectly calibrated"). Stratified: ECE 0.2. DCE emits difficulty-varying confidence (much cancellation — Claude/LifeEval pooled 0.05 → stratified 0.23); SPD emits near-flat modal mass (pooled ≈ stratified). That differential is the whole reason Claude/LifeEval flips sign.

Does the choice even matter? For 11 of 12 cells, no — both techniques give the same verdict. Exactly one cell genuinely flips direction: Claude Haiku 4.5 · LifeEval, the very cell the paper spotlighted.

So the metric debate changes one cell's sign and sharpens the diagnosis of the illusory backfires — but the bottom line is invariant: SPD is a net calibration win for overconfident models, with one small real backfire. The deep dive didn't overturn the quick read ("imperfect, but it works") — it proved which single claim is metric-contingent, and that nothing else is.

Red-team

What could break this — and why it doesn't

  • Stratified-ECE small-sample bias? Debiased each ECE against its perfect-calibration null (simulate outcomes ~ Bernoulli(conf), subtract expected bias). Every ΔECE moves ≤0.015 and no sign flips — the stratified DCE inflation is real miscalibration, not bin noise.
  • Parse dropout driving it? Lenient re-scoring uses 100% of rows and strengthens the Claude/LifeEval pooled backfire (+0.025 → +0.113); the verdict isn't an artefact of discarded data.
  • Metric cherry-picking? Both pooled and stratified are shown. The robust conclusions hold under both; only the sign of the small Claude/LifeEval effect is standard-dependent, and we say so.
  • Small n. 12 cells (4 models × 3 domains). Directional claims are robust; fine cross-domain ordering is not. Percentile bootstrap CIs, 2000 resamples, paired on item identity.
For the paper

Recommended restatement of RQ 3

SPD prompting significantly improves calibration for overconfident models — roughly halving ECE on MedEval and cutting it sharply on WGD (7 of 12 cells show large, standard-independent gains). Its effect size is governed by task structure — the gap between SPD's bin-pinned modal confidence and a domain's base-rate accuracy — not by the model's DCE calibration. The two cells reported as dramatic "backfires" are artefacts of percentage inflation, pooling cancellation, and answer-parse dropout: GPT-5.4 Mini/WGD is a null, and Claude/LifeEval worsens only marginal calibration while improving conditional calibration. The one robust degradation is small (Llama 4 Maverick/LifeEval), where SPD's modal mass turns underconfident against a high base rate.

Report absolute paired ΔECE with 95% CIs under both a pooled and a difficulty-stratified standard as the effect size; keep percent change only as secondary context.
